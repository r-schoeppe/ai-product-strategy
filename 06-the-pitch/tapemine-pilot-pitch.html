Process illustration with mock data. The opponent report runs in our first prototype; tagging, the builder and the feedback are designs. Green marks data feedback, typed as in the Module 2 flywheel: correction, preference, domain context.

Walk the three columns left to right. One: a coach tags a play from the film with a formation and a result, in seconds. Two: the opponent report turns those tags into ranked tells, each with the plays behind it; if a play does not belong, the coach removes or retags it and the tell recomputes. Three: the gameplan builder drafts calls from the coach’s own playbook, and the coach confirms or changes each one. The green labels name the feedback types from the course’s data flywheel. Correction: the user fixes the AI’s output, which is proprietary training data (removing or retagging a play, editing a call, “doesn’t fit” with a reason). Preference: every confirm or swap builds a taste profile for that coach. Domain context: the after-match debrief (worked, did not, not run) is knowledge about real 5-on-5 situations that a generic model lacks. Be clear about status: the opponent report runs in our first prototype on mock data; the tagging screen, the builder and the feedback are designed and not built. The pilot builds them during the winter break.

Why trust is the product here

The stakes are a game

A coach changes the defense because of a tell. A wrong tell costs a game.

A summary is easy to copy

What is not: evidence on every claim, and a test gate before every release.

It costs almost nothing

Code computes the numbers and the AI writes them up: €0.23 of about €3 a month.

Confidence UX: evidence, not the AI’s opinion

Some evidence · 5 of 5 plays

Not enough film yet · 3 plays

Tiers come from the lower bound (95%) of each tell’s share. Every number must match the computed value, and a 22-case test set gates releases. A plain computed report is the fallback.

Trust is the product because the report speaks for us: like the Air Canada chatbot, what the AI says is what the company said. The design answer is evidence, not a score the model gives itself. A coach can check “13 of 14 plays” and watch the clips; they cannot check “the AI is 92% sure”. 13 of 14 is strong; 5 of 5 stays at “some” because five plays could be luck; three plays is too few to say anything. The coach can change the minimum, see the plays behind any tell, and correct a call, and every correction is data. The reliability contract behind it: accuracy target 92% on the test set, hallucination under 1%, report under 4 seconds, drift under 0.5% a week, and if the narration misbehaves we automatically serve the plain computed report. These are targets we set, not measured results.

Size: about 100 teams in Europe and the US. Metric: coach engagement before the game (a confirmed sheet) and after it (debrief, tagged plays). Thresholds are a first proposal; the debrief and tagged-play numbers are placeholders. Winter 2026/27: build and sign the league. Spring 2027: pilot.

The pilot in one line: about 100 amateur teams in Europe and the US, half a season, and the critical question is whether amateur coaches engage before and after the game. Before the game: do they confirm a gameplan sheet before kickoff. After the game: do they debrief and tag plays. Price is held, so we are not testing price. Funders trust a plan that shows how it dies. The last two rows are the whole bet: coaches must plan before games and put in the data afterwards. The first two protect the money before the season starts. Timeline: nothing can be tested before the season, and half a season is the least you need to judge behavior, so everything points at one gate in mid-season 2027; the 2028 Olympic-year season is the first honest read on compounding. If asked about risks: (1) Youth film exposed or misused is the front-page risk: team data is separated by design, individual players are never identified, and a privacy review for the EU and the US is budgeted at about €5,000 and not done yet. (2) A confident wrong tell: every number must match the computed value, a 22-case test set blocks bad releases, and a plain computed report is the fallback. (3) A competitor ships flag scouting: if coaches do not plan before games, a summary is the whole product and we stop. If asked what we do not know: market size (the pilot measures use, not market size), whether the league’s files contain every play, whether €10 per team works (the pilot holds price), and verified sport statistics.
